---
title: The Argument from Eternal Truths
description: A critical examination of Augustine's argument for the existence of god from eternal truths, arguing it is at best inconclusive as an argument for Platonism and incoherent as an argument for theism.
date: 01/10/2021
draft: false
---

There is in the writings of Augustine, and other thinkers in his line, an argument for the existence of god that goes more or less as follows: we are aware of certain truths which we recognize as not only unchanging, but unchangeable. We might, for instance, imagine a sweet, red apple, and through the play of the imagination, conceive of it as blue or bitter, but some things don't permit such imaginative alterations. When we conceive of a (Euclidean) triangle, we cannot through mental effort imagine it as having fewer than three sides or as having internal angles summing to more or less than 180 degrees. So there are certain truths which are unchanging and eternal, and so not dependent on human minds. But because truth must be conceived of by *some* mind, we must have recourse to a mind which is, like these truths, unchanging and eternal–and that mind is god.

This is a tantalizing line of thought, but it seems to me to be at best an inconclusive argument for platonism, and incoherent as an argument for theism. The sticking point is on the concept of *eternal* in play. The logic of the argument dictates that "eternal" here means not simply "throughout all time," but "necessary" (and in any case, a theist of Augustine's stripe will contend that god is outside of time). So what we really have is an argument from necessary truths, where *necessary truths* are contrasted with *contingent truths*. But when understood this way, we can see that the argument is incoherent; it is a straight contradiction to say that some claim *P* is both necessarily true and contingent on god. Or to pose it as a dilemma, if *P* is necessarily true, then there's no need to invoke god, but if *P* is contingently true, then the argument doesn't get started.

Another issue is that the argument seems to conflate a belief with the object or content of a belief, something which is conveniently obscured by talking instead of truths. If some necessary truth, like [2+2=4], subsists in the mind of god, then what is going on when we think this proposition ourselves? Is that belief in the human mind numerically identical with the same belief in the mind of god? That would be a rather mysterious doctrine (and one that probably does not cohere well with most orthodoxy). Or is that just to say that the belief in a person's mind is qualitatively the same as that in god's mind–that is, having the same content? But that's just to say that a person and god are thinking about the same thing, and it doesn't follow from that that the truth of the proposition depends on god for its truth or existence.
