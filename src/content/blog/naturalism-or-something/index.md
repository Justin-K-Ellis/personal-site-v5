Most people, or most Americans, at least, are more involved in philosophical questions than they often realize. Issues revolving around feminism, race, the just distribution of wealth and resources are central to our politics, and are intrinsically tied to philosophical questions. Though probably less prominent than the topics I just mentioned, I think atheism is among them (I take atheism to be first and foremost a philosophical position, not a scientific one), especially when one considers it in relation to matters of Church and State.

Leaving aside questions of _psychological_ motivation, why do people subscribe to atheism? I suspect it is for largely one or both of two reasons: 1) they consider religious beliefs to be in and of themselves irrational, or 2) they believe that scientific evidence rules out religious positions (note that above I said that atheism is a philosophical position rather than a scientific one, but this is compatible with it being supported by propositions from science). It’s this second position I want to focus on.

Or more specifically, why are most philosophers atheists? I think that this is because most philosophers nowadays endorse some form of naturalism, and naturalism entails atheism. But what is naturalism? This is one of those questions that’s difficult to answer since the term seems to mean so many things to so many people. One popular reply is that naturalism is the stance that all of reality, all that exists, is what’s physical, or what’s revealed by empirical science to exist. Another is that science is the best guide we have to what exists. Many people hold both of these positions. [1]

I do, however, have my reservations about this characterization of naturalism. Namely, taking the metaphysical position in particular, it seems to leave out subjective, phenomenal consciousness, at least as this position is usually construed. This is because it seems committed to analyzing all of reality into the properties and relations described in fundamental physics, which to the best of my knowledge does not include what it’s like to see the color of a ripening mandarin orange, or the feeling of nostalgia. But surely these elements of subjective consciousness are as real as anything, and it would it would seem to be an oddly weak characterization of naturalism that couldn’t accommodate them. Phenomenalism, for instance, puts sensations of the ground floor of reality, but it is generally considered a form of naturalism, or at least compatible with it. Is there an alternative formulation of naturalism that doesn’t commit us to physicalism?

One intriguing suggestion come from Michael Della Rocca in his exposition of Spinoza, to the effect that all beings are bound by the same casual laws. As he puts it:

> Of course, “naturalism” can mean many different things, but by “Spinoza’s naturalism” I mean his thesis that everything in the world plays by the same rules; there are no things that are somehow connected with each other but that are not governed by the same principles.

_Spinoza_, p. 5

Right or wrong, this does seem to me an interesting avenue to explore, since it prima facia looks to avoid a commitment to the problematic physicalism I outlined above. Spinoza, of course, doesn’t reduce the mental to the physical, but see them both as attributes of _deus sive natura_.

I wonder if this definition couldn’t be tightened, however. Some theologians hold that the god they have in mind is the transcendent origin of all logically contingent existents, itself uncontingent and absolutely necessary. What about such a deity? It would seem unlikely that such as being would play by the same rules as everything else, but suppose it did? And after all, doesn’t Spinoza, who I just mentioned, conceive of his god-or-nature in similar terms? Yet one might well wonder how precisely such a being, so abstract and radically different from the world it is supposed to create and preserve, goes about explaining the world we find ourselves in. Indeed, the existence of logically necessary beings have suspect since Hume and Kant. [2]

Thus, one might define naturalism in terms of the following conditions:

1. All beings are governed by the same principles.

2. All beings are logically contingent.

Of course, 1 and 2 seem conceptually separable, and 2 is a very unspinozistic claim indeed. One might also argue that by condition 1, because the general principles that govern entities just are the laws of physics, we end up back at physicalism (see the SEP entry on the topic, especially the remarks related to casual closure). Still, 1 and 2 do appear to capture a certain intuitive sense of what naturalism is or could be without doing away with the mental. Though broader than the physicalist interpretation of naturalism, it is clearly compatible with it. It is also compatible with forms of idealism, panpsychism, and neutral monism–which strikes me as a virtue. One might call this stance broad naturalism, or something like that.

Note 1: It is a by now fairly shopworn objection that holding both of these is circular, since science can only be the best guide to showing us what exists if we assume that only the physical exists, which is supposed to be justified by claiming that we know that only the physical exists, since that is all that is revealed by science, which is our best guide to what exists. Notice, however, that question begging only enters the picture if both propositions need the other to be justified. There’s no circular reasoning if one or both proposition is independently justified.

Note 2: Consider, in addition to his claim that being cannot be a property of anything, this point made by Kant:

> If the supreme being stood in this chain of conditions, then it would itself be a member of the series of these conditions; and thus it would–like the lower members to which it is superior–still require further investigation considering its own, still higher basis. On the other hand, if one wants to separate the supreme being from this chain and keep it–as a merely intelligible being–from also being included in the series of natural causes, then just what bridge can reason build so as to reach this being? For all laws of the transition from effects to causes–indeed, all synthesis and expansion of our cognition as such–pertain to nothing but possible experience; hence the pertain merely to objects of the world of sense and can have signification only in regard to them.

_Critique of Pure Reason_, A621/B649

Which I take to mean that the theist who wishes to explain the empirical, contingent world with the help of a deity is faced with a dilemma. Either that being is a part of the casual series of the world and consequently requires an antecedent cause itself, a being some theologians would dismiss as a mere craftsman-god, or else its relationship to the contingent, empirical world is simply unintelligible.

Kant is making a ontological point here, but consider a related epistemological one made by the Christian philosopher-apologist Richard Swinburne. Swinburne eschews what he calls in this connection an “absolute explanation.”

> I do not believe that there can be any absolute explanations of logically contingent phenomena. For surely never does anything explain itself. P‘s existence at t2 may be explained in part by P‘s existence as t1, But P‘s existence at t1 could not explain P‘s existence at t1. P‘s existence at t1 might be the ultimate brute fact about the universe, but it would not explain itself. Nor can anything logically necessary provide any explanation of anything logically contingent. For a full explanation is, as we have seen, such that the explanandum (that is, the phenomenon requiring explanation) is deducible from it. **But you cannot deduce anything logically contingent from anything logically necessary.**

_The Existence of God,_ Chapter 4 (emphasis mine)

Which strikes me as a reasonable point. What among the truths of arithmetic shows how many oranges there are on my table, or what in the laws of logic demonstrates if it’s a weekday or a weekend, or how would you know just from the definition of bachelor if Crates is married or not? In general, if you somehow had complete knowledge of what logical, mathematical, and conceptual truths there are, what factual knowledge could you discover from them alone? Seemingly nothing, and if no contingent truth follows from any necessary truth (assuming those two categories are exhaustive), then any logically contingent truth must follow from another logically contingent truth, and so on. Swinburne of course wants us to terminate this regress in an almighty, all good, disembodied, though logically contingent, spirit, but taking that claim up is a different matter, and this footnote is entirely too long as it is, so I won’t pursue the topic further here.

Of course, many theists would prefer to disagree with his position that god is an ultimate but logically contingent being. After all, what is the point, they might ask, if god is just one element in the universe among many? Perhaps for present purposes we should, pace Swinburne, agree with them and stick with keeping contingency among our criterion for naturalism.
