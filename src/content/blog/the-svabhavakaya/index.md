I think that there’s a tendency in western Buddhism studies, and especially it seems to me among those developing naturalized Buddhism, to emphasize _shunyata_ (emptiness)–that is, the rejection of the existence of _svabhava_ (essence, substance, self-nature, etc.). Now, this is of course with justification given how central a concept it is in the tradition, but it often comes with the neglect of the **Buddha-nature** idea, as something truly real and existent.

In both the east and west, there seem to be two ways of squaring emptiness with Buddha-nature. One is to take the tact that canonical Buddha-nature works like the _Srimala Sutra_ and the _Awakening of Faith_, and say that emptiness properly applies only to mundane, conditioned things, while applying to the Buddha-nature/dharmakaya in the sense that it is empty of phenomenal, conditioned dharmas. ‘…the true Mind is eternal, permanent, immutable, pure and self-sufficient; therefore, it is called “nonempty”,’ to take a fairly representative quote from the _Awakening_. The other is to say that _shunyata_ really is the highest teaching, and Buddha-nature is simply taught to attract those who would at first be repelled by emptiness.

As it happens, I was thumbing through DT Suzuki’s _Studies in the Lankavatara Sutra_ and noticed that according to him, as less common synonym for _dharmakaya_ (法身) is _svabhavakaya_ (自性身). That is, the svabhaya-body. It would certainly seem to me that, whatever the leanings of other Buddhist thinkers (to say nothing of the historical Buddha himself), there were those who understood the highest reality (the _dharmakaya_) not, as is sometimes said, as a positive expression of _shunyata_, but as _svabhaya_.
