## Thoughts and Reflections

As should be clear enough, this year was heavy with Early Modern philosophy and books about it–save for two books on Epicureanism (*De Rerum Natura* and *The Swerve*), which is arguably an important antecedent to Enlightenment thought, and Aristotle's *Metaphysics* and a volume of Frederick Copleston's history of philosophy, which I read in anticipation of a run of mediaeval philosophy in early 2020.

Standout works this year include Locke's *Essay*, a book I've been meaning to read for a very long time now (side note: my Early Modern Philosophy course in college was rather eccentrically structured and focused a lot on developments in early modern science, which meant a lot of stuff like the Leibniz-Clarke debate and no Locke or Hume, if you can believe it; this is a gap in my knowledge I've been trying to fill as of late, as you can see). The book is incredibly long (721 pages in my Nidditch edition), and, like not a few philosophy books from this period (or any), tedious at times (the early modern philosophers seem to have had an enthusiasm for analyzing the concept of space which I do not share), but also tremendously thought-provoking. This all but goes without saying of course, given the work's classic status, but I know I'll be returning to it again.

Also well worthy of mention is Bayle's *Dictionary*, a pretty amazing tour de force of erudition and dialectic. I can't say that this book is as well known as Locke's *Essay*, and I have to admit that I knew next to nothing about Bayle or the *Dictionary* when I was studying philosophy as an undergrad (save for perhaps the odd reference here or there when studying Leibniz). This is really a shame, as the book, though sometimes dense and lengthy even in the abridged translation I read, is an incredibly engaging work.

Bayle's *Dictionary* was extremely influential and, from what I can tell, nearly every intellectual during The Enlightenment was reading it, including Berkeley and Hume, both of who seemed to take on important ideas from it. The argument in Berkeley's *Principles* that we can't, *pace* Locke, intelligibly separate ideas of primary qualities (like shape and motion) from ideas of secondary qualities (like color and smell) is found in the *Dictionary*, and one suspects that Bayle's discussion of the problem of evil influenced Hume's take in his *Dialogues* (an old favorite of mine).

Which brings me to the next landmark of my 2019 readings, Hume's *Treatise*. It's not as elegantly written as his *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, and I must confess that I am among those who find Book 1 to be the most interesting, but I can also say that its canonical status is earned. It is, for one thing, very impressive in its scope, covering a theory of ideas, space and time, the nature of causation, probability, and induction, skepticism, personal identity, a theory of emotions and moral psychology, the free will debate, metaethics, and a theory of justice. The works is packed with clever and penetrating arguments that should delight and provoke anyone with an interest in philosophy, though I suppose I don't need to sing the praises of a book that's been out for hundreds of years. Hume's method is at once naturalistic and skeptical, an approach markedly different from the theocentric one employed by Hume's contemporaries like Leibniz and Berkeley, and for that matter the majority of his predecessors. Only the Epicureans, Hobbes, and (somewhat controversially) Spinoza had been as explicitly naturalistic.

This brings me to the last book I want to touch on, Jonathan Israel's *Radical Enlightenment*. I've discussed this work a bit in a previous post, but I can't avoid mentioning it here. Israel's general thesis is that Spinoza's philosophy, though sometimes treated as a detour in the history of philosophy, a difficult if ultimately insignificant exercises of metaphysical systematics in a pre-Kantian age, had a deep and pervasive impact on the history and development of the Enlightenment. In the previous paragraph I used the phrase "somewhat controversially" in reference to Spinoza to signal that there might be something odd about claiming Spinoza's system as naturalistic. If naturalism is, after all, contrary to theocentrism, and God is the linchpin of Spinoza's entire philosophical edifice, isn't it a mistake to describe his system as naturalistic? But things are not so simple. Spinoza's concept of God is, if this is not a contradiction to say, naturalistic. Unlike the traditional god of theism, which transcends the cosmos and creates it as a free act, Spinoza's God is the immanent substance of the universe, and finite, contingent things are inseparable from it. Where the god of theism creates the world for a reason and has a plan for it, Spinoza's God has no purposes and imposes no ends on things. Likewise, the traditional theocentric view invites a picture where man is somewhere between god and nature; Spinoza urges that the human is not "a kingdom within a kingdom," operating with its own semi-divine ways, but is a part of nature and obeys its laws. Israel argues that Spinoza's radical naturalism, and accompanying theories of tolerance and republican, secular government, were central to the Enlightenment, predating and influencing better known figures. *Radical Enlightenment* certainly taught me a lot about a lot of figures I had never heard of and is extremely well documented (the bibliography alone runs some hundred pages).
