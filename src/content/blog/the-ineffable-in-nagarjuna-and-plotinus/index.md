> "However many beings there are in whatever realms of beings might exist, whether they are born from an egg, born from a womb, born from the water or the air, whether they have form or no form, whether they have perception or no perception or neither perception nor no perception, in whatever conceivable realm of being one might conceive of beings, in the realm of complete nirvana I shall liberate them all. And though I thus liberate countless beings, not a single being is liberated."
>
> — Diamond Sutra

_Ineffability_ is a concept more often associated with mysticism than philosophy. It tends to conjure up images of divine revelation and ecstatic union, not investigation and analysis. On the other hand, one might well argue that first-person, phenomenal experiences are in a way ineffable, or at least unintelligible without direct acquaintance (how would you, say, describe the taste of pineapple to an alien with no sense of taste or smell?). But my purpose here isn't to defend or criticize the legitimacy of the idea of ineffability. Rather, I want to do some comparative philosophy. In particular, I want to compare and contrast ineffability as it appears in the second century Buddhist philosopher Nagarjuna and the third century Neoplatonist philosopher Plotinus.

But first, what is it to be ineffable? And what is it that's ineffable? For the purposes of this post, I'll use the term to mean the inability to describe something (with words or otherwise). As will become apparent as we proceed, Nagarjuna and Plotinus both believe that the ultimate reality is ineffable, and both believe this to be the case because we cannot attribute predicates to this ultimate reality. But as will also become apparent, Nagarjuna and Plotinus have very different ideas about just what the nature of this reality is.

In Plotinus, ineffability is essentially a consequence of the ultimate reality's simplicity. For the uninitiated, Plotinus presents us with a metaphysical cosmology that's hierarchical and anti-materialist, either marvelous or wearying, depending on your disposition, and in any case complex (well, complex for most of us; it's relatively simple compared to the worlds of many of Plotinus' successors). Starting from the bottom, we have formless matter, an idea more or less inherited from Aristotelianism, and above that, embodied forms–everyday things, so far as we're concerned. Above material objects, Plotinus says, are souls, and among souls, rational souls (such as ours) are on top. The best soul of them all, above the souls of mundane creatures, is the soul of the world, and beyond that still is the _Nous_: pure, immaterial intellect or understanding. It is the realm of Being and is the seat of Plato's Ideas. And above the _Nous_, beyond Being, is the first principle of everything, Plotinus' ultimate reality: the One.

Plotinus reasons his way to the One more or less as follows: for anything _to be_ is for it _to be one_, to have some kind of unity. A group of armed people is only an army in virtue of its unity, a certain amount of flesh and blood is _an animal_ thanks to its unity, and so on. Purportedly immaterial beings, like souls, don't have physical parts to unify but do undergo alteration in virtue of the flow of thoughts. The Ideas, like beauty and justice, are unchanging but subject to multiple predicates (any given form is the _same_ as itself, _different_ from other forms, has _being_, and so on). An important thing to recognize is that in Plotinus' scheme, beings receive their being from those realities above them: Ideas dispense unity upon souls, souls upon bodies, and so on. To Plotinus' thinking, there must be some unity _per se_, above even the timeless, immaterial _Nous_, where the unifying begins: the One. This One is unity pure and simple; it has no physical parts or mental alterations, nor even any predicates.

This is where the ineffability comes in. The thought is that the One has no properties or predicates ("intelligibles," _noeton_, in Plotinus' idiom), as that would introduce plurality and thus violate its absolute simplicity. A red apple is in a way two things, itself and red. But the One cannot be two or otherwise multiple even in this way. Thus, it in principle cannot be a subject of predication. The predicate _large_ does not apply to it, so we cannot say, "The One is large;" _wise_ does not apply to it, so we cannot say, "The One is wise;" _beauty_ does not apply to it, so we cannot say, "The One is beautiful." And because it has no predicates, there's nothing we can say about it. Hence the ineffability of the One.

> "But we in our travail do not know what we ought to say, and are speaking of what cannot be spoken, and give it a name because we want to indicate it to ourselves best we can. Perhaps this name 'One' contains only a denial of multiplicity."
>
> — Ennead V.5.6

Plotinus also gives an argument like this: for Plotinus, material objects are grasped by the senses, and the objects of knowledge, the objects of the intellect, are the immaterial Ideas or forms that proceed from the One. But since the One is the cause of forms, it is not itself a form. So, the One cannot be known by the intellect.

> "Since the substance which is generated from the One is form–one could not say that what is generated from that source is anything else–and not the form of some one thing but of everything, so that no form is left outside it, the One must be without form."
>
> — Ennead V.5.6

Such, Plotinus.

What is it about Nagarjuna's conception of the ultimate reality and what makes it ineffable? As I suggested above, there are contrasts–indeed, significant contrasts–between how reality is understood by Plotinus and Nagarjuna. Perhaps I can set the stage by continuing the quotation from the famously paradoxical _Diamond Sutra_ I began this essay with.

> "And why not? Subhuti, a bodhisattva who creates that perception of a being cannot be called a 'bodhisattva.' And why not? Subhuti, no one can be called a bodhisattva who creates a perception of a self or who creates the perception of a being, a life, or a soul."
>
> — Diamond Sutra

What is the meaning of such a paradoxical statement? What could the Buddha possibly meaning by saying that though the bodhisattva vows to liberate all sentient beings, in truth no beings are liberated? The answer lies, it seems to me, in the Buddhist doctrine of two truths, something Nagarjuna makes important use of. The short of it is that there are two truths, two ways of seeing the nature of reality. The first is the conventional truth, the ordinary view of the world that conceptualizes things as separate objects with discreet natures. The ultimate truth is that of emptiness (_shunyata_).

Emptiness of what? Emptiness of _atman_ (self) or empty of _svabhava_ (substance, essence). _Svabhava_, which I glossed as "substance" or "essence," literally means "self-nature," and is also often translated as "inherent existence." By this Nagarjuna especially seems to mean something that exists unconditionally. One of the major themes in Nagarjuna's _Mulamadhyamakakarika_ (MMK) is to argue against the applicability of _svabhava_. _Svabhava_, Nagarjuna argues throughout the work, is an incoherent notion, and even if it weren't, we can make no sense of how it would relate to contingent phenomena. Rather, all things exist conditionally. For things to exist through causes and conditions is just for them to lack _svabhava_, to be empty (_shunya_).

This should not be taken to mean, however, that nothing exists. The two truths, after all, are both truths. Everyday things do exist, but their existence is as part of the stream of causes and conditions; they are dependently originated. To be a bodhisattva, an enlightened being, is to both endeavor to liberate suffering beings out of compassion and to have the wisdom to see that they are not selves or souls standing behind or above the phenomena–and thus to see that there are no selves or souls to liberate.

Moreover, we should not take any of this to mean that emptiness is some kind of hypercosmic substance, above and beyond the phenomena, in its own realm. Rather, emptiness is just the emptiness of things, the flux of conditioned existence that is _samsara_. But what about _nirvana_, something that is often contrasted with _samsara_ and could claim to be the ultimate reality in Buddhism? It is said by Buddhists to be neither changing nor dependent. But this too, says Nagarjuna, is dependent origination seen right. In emptiness there are no _svabhava_ at all, and those non-existent _svabhava_ do not, as it were, change or depend on anything.

> "There is not the slightest difference
> Between _samsara_ and _nirvana_.
> There is not the slightest difference
> Between _nirvana_ and _samsara_."
>
> — Mulamadhyamakakarika 25.19

This brings us back to the root of ineffability in Plotinus. For Plotinus, we cannot speak of the One beyond being because we cannot predicate anything of it. Similarly with Nagarjuna, we cannot deploy our subject-predicate assertions, so useful in the realm of the conventional, since from the ultimate point of view, there are no subjects to be predicated.

> "One who grasps the view that the Tathagata exists,
> Having seized the Buddha,
> Constructs conceptual fabrications
> About one who has achieved nirvana.
> Since he is by nature empty,
> The thought that the Buddha
> Exists or does not exist
> After nirvana is not appropriate."
>
> — Mulamadhyamakakarika 22.13-16

> "Those who develop mental fabrications with regard to the Buddha,
> Who has gone beyond all fabrications,
> As a consequence of those cognitive fabrications,
> Fail to see the Tathagata."
>
> — Mulamadhyamakakarika 22.13-16

> "Whatever is the essence of the Tathagata,
> That is the essence of the world.
> The Tathagata has no essence.
> The world is without essence."
>
> — Mulamadhyamakakarika 22.13-16

In summary, Nagarjuna and Plotinus both argue that the ultimate reality is ineffable. They do this for similar reasons: namely the impossibility of predicating anything of the ultimate reality. However, the identity of this ultimate reality is very different for these thinkers. For Plotinus, it is the transcendent One beyond being; for Nagarjuna, it is this world of interdependent causes and effects.
