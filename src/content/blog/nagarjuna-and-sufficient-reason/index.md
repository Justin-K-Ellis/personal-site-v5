I always find it interesting when philosophers employ the same principles to arrive at significantly different conclusions. One philosophical idea that was much deployed in the Early Modern period is the *principle of sufficient reason* (PSR), which states, more or less, that for any phenomenon or state of affairs, there must some reason which sufficiently accounts for why the fact in question obtains. It embodies what Jonathan Bennett has called *explanatory rationalism*; that every why-question must in principle have an answer. 'Why is it so hot?'–'Because it's noon in summer and the sun is out.' 'Why did the glass break?'–'Because there was an earthquake and it fell off the table,' etc. Whether the PSR is true or not (and there's reason to think it isn't) is a question in itself, but I leave that aside from now.

The PSR is of course most famously associated with Leibniz. As he put it at *Monadology* 31-32:

> 31. Our reasonings are found on two great principles: the *principle of contradiction*…
> 32. And that of *sufficient reason*, in virtue of which we hold that no fact could every be true or existent, no statement correct, unless there were a sufficient reason why and not otherwise–even though those reasons will usually not be knowable by us.

The PSR is also often specified in terms of causation. Hume gives a version that goes:

> 'Tis a general maxim in philosophy, that *whatever begins to exist, must have a cause of existence*. This is commonly taken for granted in all reasonings, without any proof given or demanded.
>
> *Treatise* 1.3.3

Hume was of course famously skeptical of just how intuitively certain the PSR really was. Spinoza displayed no such doubts, though like Hume he articulates it in terms of causation. He also gives a kind of double PSR, claiming that not only does every phenomenon require a cause to account for it, but that any given cause will necessarily produce an effect:

> From a determinate cause there necessarily follows an effect; on the other hand, if there be no determinate cause, it is impossible that an effect should follow.
>
> *Ethics*, IA3

(Why believe the first half of this is true? I think the reasoning may be something like this: if we say that *A causes B*, then this proposition is either necessarily true, and the causal relationship between A and B holds necessarily, where an A will always give rise to a B (thus vindicating Spinoza's axiom), or this proposition is contingent (sometimes As causes Bs, but sometimes they don't). In that case, by the PSR, we need something, a sufficient reason, to account for this A causing a B. Perhaps some C that will guarantee A to cause B. So we'd have something like *C causes A to cause B*. But now we seem to be back where we started, where we have to ask what relationship C stands to A causing B. Either somewhere this bottoms out in a necessary causal relationship, or we have an infinite regress of explanation and the PSR is never satisfied. But I digress.)

In any case, the second century Buddhist philosopher Nagarjuna seems to have used a double PSR like Spinoza's. At *Mulamadhyamakakarika* 4.2-3, he writes:

> If apart from the cause of the form, there were form,
> Form would be without cause.
> But nowhere is there an effect
> Without a cause.
>
> If apart from form
> There were a cause of form,
> It would be a cause without an effect.
> But there are no causes without effects.

Nagarjuna's overall goal is to demonstrate that all phenomenon are empty of inherent existence, meaning that they are radically contingent and need conditions to exist–the theory of *pratityasamutpada* or dependent origination. To that end, Nagarjuna is in the chapter at hand examining the conditions under which form<sup>1</sup>, one kind of principle Buddhist *skandha* ("aggregate") is said to arise. In the more immediate context, the idea seems to be that (1) nothing comes into existence without a cause, so form must have a cause, and (2), when sufficient conditions are present, an effect is necessarily produced, so when the causes of form are present, so too will be form.

As I mentioned above, sometimes very different thinkers employ similar principles. Leibniz's distinction between truths of reason and truths of fact closely parallels Hume's fork between relations of ideas and matters of fact, and Hume and Leibniz hardly come to the same conclusions about how the world works. Closer to today's topic, Leibniz and Spinoza arguably<sup>2</sup> both make the PSR central to their metaphysics–but while Leibniz attempts to defend something closer to the traditional, Christian, theocentric worldview, Spinoza sets out a radical and immanentist ontology. And here with Nagarjuna, a philosopher far removed from the debates of early modern philosophy in both time and space, we something that would be familiar to philosopher of the Enlightenment.

---

**Note 1**: "Form" here renders the Sanskrit *rupa*, which, as the translator of and commentator on the work, Jay Garfield, notes, really means "matter." It does not, so far as I know, bring in connotations of *eidos* and *morphe* as the term "form" typically does in western philosophy. The classical Sino-Japanese translation of *rupa*, by the way, is 色, which literally means "color."

**Note 2**: Michael Della Rocca, for example, argues that the PSR is central to Spinoza's analysis in the *Ethics*.
