I’ve recently finished reading Jonathan Israel’s masterful *Radical Enlightenment: Philosophy and the Making of Modernity 1650 – 1750*, a book which, as its title suggests, is a study of the Early Enlightenment, with emphasis on the radical thread of that movement. What’s so interesting about this work, at least to someone like myself who knows more about philosophical arguments than historical context, is the presentation of just how fraught a conflict the spread of ideas in this period was. So often, especially at the introductory level, we study philosophers and their arguments in abstraction from their historical contexts. I don’t think this is a bad thing per se, but it leaves out a lot of background information which can be useful in interpreting the ideas in play. Just as importantly, this context is really, really interesting (at least to me).

As Israel lays it out, there were essentially three intellectual camps in play in this period: the traditionalists, the moderate enlightenment, and the radical enlightenment camps, and their relative positions in some ways mirrored camps in our contemporary political situation, with conservatives opposing centrists opposing leftists. The traditionalists, most closely aligned with institutionalized confessional Christianity, tended to subscribe to the Aristotelian-Scholastic philosophy, the radicals (according to Israel’s thesis) were centered on Spinoza’s naturalistic, republican philosophy, while the moderate enlightenment was actually composed of three different factions. These were the Cartesians and Malebranchists, those who subscribed to the Newton-Locke system, and the Leibniz-Wolffians.

Part of what makes what Israel calls the Moderate Enlightenment interesting is both its double-sided nature of and its evolution and internal competition. By “double-sided nature” I mean how it was at once perceived as a threat to the orthodoxy and and as a bulwark against the Radical wing of the Enlightenment, and by “evolution and internal competition” I mean how the above factions were in competition with one another. One can’t help but be amazed at how Cartesianism was both met with massive resistance from the establishment but rapidly and seemingly inexorably spread through the academic world of the time, only to be challenged by and eventually replaced by Newtonian-Lockean or Leibniz-Wolffian ideas. Cartesianism was both used to buttress the traditional theocentric world view (any reader of Descartes’ *Meditations* and *Objections and Replies* knows how much theology there is in those pages) and was attacked by traditionalists as not only being sufficiently unorthodox in and of itself, but as the nest from which the reviled Spinozism had sprung. Likewise, the ideas of Locke and Leibniz were seen as weapons against the dangers of Cartesianism and Spinozism, while themselves being attacked by traditionalists as being too naturalistic.

Figures like Newton and Locke are to some degree culture heroes, at least for many of us, and not for no reason. Their intellectual achievements were considerable and I wouldn’t suggest that Newton is undeserving of his reputation in physics or Locke in metaphysics and epistemology. However, what Israel wishes to underline is how much of the thinking on liberty and democracy we take to be characteristic of these moderate Enlightenment figures is at best muted by our standards. Leibniz, like many of his contemporaries, was in favor censorship to regulate subversive and naturalistic ideas, and the tolerance advocated by Locke to a large degree amounted to freedom of worship for Protestant sects (good luck if you’re a Catholic or, have mercy, an atheist.) But it was in Spinoza’s groundbreaking *Tractatus Theologico-Politicus* that we find argument for freedom of speech and freedom of thought (*libertas philosophandi*) as we would recognize it now. As Spinoza puts it toward the end of his theological-political treatise:

> The state can pursue no safer course than to regard piety and religion as consisting solely in the exercise of charity and just dealing, and that the right of the sovereign, both in religious and secular spheres, should be restricted to men’s actions, with everyone being allowed to think what he will and to say what he thinks.

This was a radical idea in Spinoza’s time, and for some in our day, it still is.

***

**Note 1:** Lockean empiricism and natural philosophy was after all a part of the mechanical philosophy, and has some clear affinities with naturalism, and Locke was even (quite implausibly) attacked as a secret follower of Spinoza. Christian Wolff, despite being one of the more articulate critics of Spinoza, was himself accused of being a crypto-Spinozist, and was at one point in his career literally run out of town by reactionary opponents.
