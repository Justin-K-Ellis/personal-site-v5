This post is going to be expository rather than argumentative. That is, I'm going to try and get clear about a couple of concepts rather than try to convince you that some claim is true or false. Producing arguments for or against a claim is a big part of what makes philosophy fun, of course, but to my mind clarifying ideas is just as important.

In Naturalism, or Something Like That, I laid down two criteria for what would make a philosophical perspective naturalistic and noted a tension in my formulation with a philosophy that is arguably naturalistic but sits poorly with the criteria there. In this post I want to further explore those criteria and try to relieve the tension with a distinction.

The first criteria I mentioned there was the idea that (a) *all beings are governed by the same principles*. Spinoza gives expression to this idea when he says:

> "Nature is always the same, and its force and power of acting is everywhere one and the same; that is, the laws and rules of Nature according to which all things happen and change from one form to another are everywhere and always the same."
>
> *Ethics*, Part III, Preface

The second criteria was that (b) *all beings are logically contingent*. This can be seen in the (theist) character Cleanthes' claim in Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* that:

> "It is pretended, that the deity is a necessarily existent being...The words, therefore, *necessary existence*, have no meaning; or, which is the same thing, none that is consistent."
>
> *Dialogues*, 9.6

As the title of this post and these quotes indicate, I'm associating these propositions, (a) and (b), with Spinoza and Hume. However, I have to be careful about what I am and am not trying to claim. Based on the forgoing, one might think that I'm saying that a Spinozist naturalism will accept (a), but not (b), while a Humean naturalism will endorse (b) and not (a). I think Hume would probably accept (a), and at any rate, the Humean naturalism I want to float wouldn't deny (a). Rather, the contrast I want to draw out is that a Spinozist naturalism would accept that all beings are governed by the same natural laws while rejecting the claim that all beings are logically or metaphysically contingent–i.e., the idea that there are no necessary beings–while the Humean will say that we can't validly apply the concept of *necessity* to individuals, including god.

My language has implied that I'm not trying to provide exact reconstructions of the philosophies of Spinoza and Hume but rather articulate ways of thinking that capture important aspects of their thought. Spinoza and Hume are often presented as being on opposite ends of early modern philosophy, Spinoza representing a pure rationalism and Hume illustrating an extreme empiricism. This is not without a grain of truth or two, but this shouldn't cause us neglect their being united by a commitment to naturalism. Still, if there is to be a meaningful contrast here, I think it would be on this point. The Humean denies that there are any necessary beings and the Spinozist says that there is exactly one: Nature.
