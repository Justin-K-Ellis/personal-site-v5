---
title: "Neque Concipi Potest"
description: "God, the world, and conceivability"

---

It is sometimes said by theists that, not only is god the cause of the universe, but that _nothing can even be conceived without god_. At least on the traditional interpretation of theism, where god is thought of as a substance distinct from the universe, who brings it into being freely and _ex nihilo_, I think this is exactly the wrong conclusion to draw from the idea that god is the cause of the universe. I say this because I think that the very application of the notion of cause to god and _effect_ to the universe entails this.

My argument is that if we say that some X is the cause of Y, then X and Y must be really distinct from one another. By “really distinct” I mean that one thing can exist independently of another. For instance, my laptop and my coffee cup are really distinct because the two are independent existents, while the curve of my cup’s rim cannot exist independently of the cup’s material. We can of course separate in thought the cup from its shape, but this is an abstraction (a “distinction of reason,” as they sometimes say). The curve of the porcelain and the rim are not proper parts of the cup; you can’t take them apart and lay them side by side. Or to look at it from another perspective, what philosophers call accidents or modes are not are really distinct from the substances or objects they belong to. The weight of my computer isn’t really distinct from the computer, and so on.

One point I want to make here is that it seems we don’t apply the concepts of _cause_ and _effect_ to things unless they’re really distinct. For instance, it would seem to be an abuse of terms to say that the weight of my computer is an effect of the computer itself, which is the cause. At any rate, the concept of effect certainly can’t apply to a mode or accident in the questions at hand, where _ex hypothesi_ god and the universe are supposed to be really distinct (i.e., where god is one substance and the universe is another, or rather, a collection of substances, on the conventional understanding).

Now, two things being real distinct implies that they can exist independently of one another. So this is all to say that if god is the cause of the universe, then, as any pious theist will affirm, god can exist independently of the universe. But by the same token, the universe can exist independently of god. Or to put it another way, we wind up with the proposition: _If it’s possible god can exist without the universe, then it’s possible the universe can exist without god_.

The contrapositive of this proposition is, by the way: _if it’s not possible that the universe can exist without god, then it’s not possible that god can exist without the universe, which is perhaps not so far in force and implication from quicquid est, in Deo est, et nihil sine esse, neque concipi potest_ (Spinoza, _Ethics_, Part I, Proposition 15).
