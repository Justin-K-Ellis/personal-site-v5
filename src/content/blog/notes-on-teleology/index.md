---
title: Notes on Teleology
description: A short philosophical argument that teleology—the view that nature has inherent purposes—is paradoxical and cannot overcome Hume's Is-Ought problem.
date: 01/23/2022
draft: false
---

Teleology, the idea that there are inherent purposes in nature, strikes me as paradoxical. It seems to be committed to saying that something (or someone) should be such-and-such because of the essence it possesses (or at least, this is how many versions of it go). This position arose in ancient Greek philosophy, especially Plato and Aristotle, and was predominant in the middle ages; some today believe that it overcomes Hume's Is-Ought problem, but I don't see how.

Let's represent something having an essence like this: F(x) [that is, x is F]. Either F(x) is the case for something a or it is not. Either F(a) or not-F(a). If F(a), then there's no question about if a should be F; it already is. But if not-F(a), then the normativity that's supposed to come from F disappears. If something doesn't fall under essence F, then what sense can there be in saying that it's a good F or a bad F?

Suppose someone where trying to define the essence of being Japanese, and they suggested that the essence of being Japanese involves appreciating cherry blossoms (this is something of a toy definition of course, but that doesn't matter for our purposes). Now take a hypothetical person, Sara, and ask if she is truly Japanese using this definition—asking if she appreciates cherry blossoms, bearing in mind that "truly Japanese" will include the notion of being a good Japanese person. Sara is either appreciates cherry blossoms or she doesn't. If she does, then there's no question of whether she is Japanese or not; ex hypothesi, it's a conceptual truth that she is. Inquiring if she is Japanese would be like asking "Yes, but does this triangle have three sides?" On the other hand, if she doesn't care for cherry blossoms, then that rules out her being Japanese, and so asking if she is a good Japanese person, or a true Japanese person, etc., is a moot point.

The only way out of this would seem to be that being an F entails also having some property G (say, being a knife means being sharp, so being a good knife means being a sharp knife). But this would appear to amount to saying "It is the case that F(a). Therefore, it ought to be the case that F(a) & G(a)," but this only brings us back around to the Is-Ought problem.
